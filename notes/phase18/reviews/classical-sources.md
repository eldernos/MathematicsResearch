## Positive Taylor groups, F1 and F2

Write $a_m=1/(m2^m)$ and $m=d2^j$ with $d$ odd. For a finite group, *completion* refers to its last original index. *Retirement* refers to the first shift that makes its dyadic sum integral.

1. **Periods, partition and retirement.** Put $t=\operatorname{ord}_d(2)=2^a u$. For an eventual period $P$, subtracting the congruence for $j$ from that for $j+1$ gives $t\mid d2^j(2^P-1)$, and substitution gives $t\mid P$. Thus $u/\gcd(u,d)\mid 2^P-1$. Conversely these conditions suffice for all $j\ge a$, and the congruence fails at $a-1$ for every such period when $a>0$. This gives F1.1, with the order modulo 1 taken as 1. Each tail progression has constant invertible residue. Up to $d-1$ unused terms from a single progression absorb each transient term. Blocks of $d$ partition the remaining progressions. For a group ending at $K$ its cleared numerator is odd, because only its last term contributes an odd summand. Dividing by odd $d$ preserves parity, so the reduced denominator is exactly $2^{d2^K+K}$. These arguments prove F1.1–F1.3 without any bound on the tested ray.
2. **Exceptional prime count.** For $0,K\in G$, the numerator modulo the prime $p$ is the positive integer $B(G)=\sum_{j\in G}2^{2^K-2^j+K-j}$ reduced modulo $p$. Its leading term and the gaps of at least two bits give $B(G)<(2/3)2^{2^K+K}$. The integers for $K=1,2,3$ are respectively $5$; $33,41$; and $1025,1281,1057,1313$. They classify *attainable* maxima. A prime can attain several maxima. Summing the number of prime factors over all endpoint-containing subsets bounds the number of primes with minimum maximum at most $k$ by $\sum_{i=1}^k2^{i-1}(2^i+i)<2^{2k+1}$. In particular $2^K+K>\log_2(3p/2)$ for every prime. Taking $k=\lfloor\log_2(\sqrt X/\log X)\rfloor$ gives $O(X/\log^2X)$ exceptions and the stated density-one delay. Direct subset enumeration gave 39 of 45, 157 of 167, and 642 of 668 odd primes still open through $N=200,1000,5000$.
3. **All odd rays.** Set $y=\sqrt N\log N$. Odd integers $d\le N$ with a prime factor $p>y$ have a unique such factor. Their number is $\sum_{y<p\le N}(N/(2p)+O(1))=(N/2)(\log 2+o(1))$ by partial summation of the prime number theorem (PNT). For $d=qp$ a finished ray has $2^K\le N/(qy)$. The corresponding integer $B_q(G)$ replaces $2^K-2^j$ in the exponent by $q(2^K-2^j)$. The same subset count gives $O(q(N/(qy))^2)$ possible $p$ for this $q$. Summing over $q\le N/y$ costs $O(N/\log N)$. The claim is a **liminf of at least $\log 2$** for the share of open initial groups in single-ray partitions. Equality and convergence to $\log 2$ are separate questions.
4. **Unrestricted partitions.** F2.1 uses an *odd* prime. The term with index 2 can be a singleton. The stronger `prime-delay.md` argument works for arbitrary cross-ray groups. If $k=\lfloor N/p\rfloor<p$, multiplying by $p$ leaves $\sum_{j\in S}1/(j2^j)$ modulo $p$, for the nonempty set $S$ of represented multiples. Its numerator over $2^kL_k$, $L_k=\operatorname{lcm}(1,\ldots,k)$, is strictly between 0 and $2^kL_k$. For $p\ge2^kL_k$ it survives. Taking $k=\lfloor c\log p\rfloor$ for $0<c<1/(1+\log2)$ proves $\liminf M(p)/(p\log p)\ge1/(1+\log2)$. The count $\gg N\log\log N/\log N$ in that note is also valid. For $j\le d\log N$ and $N/(2j)<p\le N/j$, the condition holds uniformly for sufficiently small fixed $d>0$. The marked $jp$ are distinct by $p>\sqrt N$. Summing the PNT lower bounds over $j$ counts **indices** in incomplete groups.
5. **Cross-ray separation.** With $R=v_2(\operatorname{den}S(G))$ and $m^*=\min\{m\in G:m>R\}$, the shifted head has denominator dividing $L_R$, since $2^{R-m}$ is integral for $m\le R$. The positive shifted tail must bridge a gap of at least $1/L_R$ and is less than $2^{R-m^*+1}/m^*$. Hence
   $$
   (m^*-R)\log 2 \le \psi(R)+\log 2-\log m^*,\qquad \psi(R)=\log L_R.
   $$
   This gives $R\ge(\log2/(1+\log2))m^*-o(m^*)$. The original $O(\log m^*)$ loss does not follow from $\psi(R)=R+o(R)$. The parent's revised statement and proof fix this. The unique-maximum argument and the collision congruence in F2.3 are valid. For example, indices $5,8,10$ give $7/1024$ with maximum exponent 11. Exhausting the 1,023 nonempty subsets of the first ten indices gives no drop with maximum index below 10. Thus an unrestricted group's last original index and its reduced dyadic exponent must be treated separately.
6. **The three cutoff strategies.** Completed groups omit a term with a prime index $(1/2+o(1))N$ just above $N/2$, so their **unwrapped** shifted error is at least $2^{(1/2-o(1))N}$. Adding any completed dyadic sum preserves all odd denominator valuations. The unfinished pieces jointly have odd denominator $\exp(N+o(N))$ at the original cutoff. Alternatively, all primes between $CN/\log N$ and $N$ survive the subset test above when $C>1+\log2$, and the upper bound is $L_N$. Closing every group touching the prefix gives the stated tail bound. Choosing the largest odd prime below $N$ in the unrestricted delay estimate even gives $M_N\gg N\log N$. A dyadic approximant's orbit eventually reaches zero. Its finite initial digits can still exhibit cancellation. F2.4 rules out applying a growing odd-denominator orbit estimate by those three routes. It does not decide the digits of the selected approximants.

## Functional identities and deterministic remainders, F3 and F4

**Horizontal rays.** For a finite set $S$ of odd rays, the completed prefix at shift $n$ is on the grid of denominator $D=\operatorname{lcm}(S)$. Outside $O_S((\log N)^2)$ positions the next insertion is at least $2\log_2 N$ away. The positive tail is then $O_S(N^{-2})$. Every empirical limit is supported on the finite grid, and thus has zero metric entropy. Intervals strictly inside a grid gap give the asserted discrepancy bound (indeed a liminf of at least $1/D$ by exhausting the gap). This proves upper point entropy zero. If claiming zero entropy of the entire orbit closure, there is also a uniform block argument. Each ray has $O(\log(L+2))$ insertions in **any** interval of length $L$, since successive gaps grow at least geometrically up to an additive constant. A block of $L$ digits is specified, up to $O_S(1)$ possible final carries, by the starting grid residue and the labelled insertion positions in the block. The shifted tail beyond the end is bounded by $O_S(1)$. This gives at most $\exp(O_S(\log^2(L+2)))$ distinct blocks. Delete the table's claim that the infinite sum “escapes determinism”. The status of $\log 2$ is open. The fixed-$p$ vertical decomposition establishes individual normality. The finite joint conclusion cited from Track D applies to its **fresh distinct prime** construction. The Phase 17 Legendre results also qualify F3's historical discussion of precision windows for $\log 2$.

**Complement.** Removing $P$-smooth indices removes no multiples of $p\notin P$. Multiplication by $p$ and reduction at $p^2>N$ gives exactly the stated truncated Frobenius sum. For each fixed band $v=\lfloor N/p\rfloor\ge1$ it is a positive rational up to the unit $2^N$, so only finitely many primes can annihilate it. Exhaustion of fixed bands and the lcm upper bound give rate 1. The extra condition involving $V_p$ in F4.1 has no definition there. A sufficient local hypothesis for the same residue is deletion of **none** of $p,2p,\ldots,\lfloor N/p\rfloor p$. Merely deleting few prime indices allows deletion of composite multiples and can change the residue. The rate alone does extend to deletions containing $o(N/\log N)$ primes: outside these primes the surviving subset in each fixed band contains $j=1$, and there are just finitely many positive rational subset sums in that band. The script tests vanishing versus nonvanishing modulo $p$. A separate exact comparison of the two residues agreed in all 176 prime/cutoff/support cases tested there. F4.2 follows since the difference of two deterministic remainders is deterministic, while a binary-normal $x$ fails to preserve normality upon addition to the normal input $-x$.

**Differential-algebra obstruction.** The working note `functional-obstruction.md` proves the proposed obstruction for all five generating functions. A finite product of fixed derivatives of the generators has polylogarithmically many possible exponents up to $N$ by counting tuples. A polynomial clears the finitely many rational coefficient denominators. In a polynomial multiple of $\operatorname{Li}_s$, a nonzero coefficient of the multiplier gives a distinct pole in the shifted coefficient rational function. For BBP the pole positions are $t-a/8$, distinct for the four slots and distinct shifts. For the exponential, multiplication by $n!$ gives a nonzero combination of falling factorials of distinct degrees. For a nonintegral binomial and the inverse-central-binomial Apéry series, coefficient ratios at fixed shifts are rational functions of $n$. Identical cancellation would make the generating function rational. A fractional order at 1 excludes this for the binomial. At $X=-4$ the Apéry function is bounded but the terms of its second derivative have the same sign and magnitudes comparable to $n^{-1/2}$ at the endpoint. This excludes a rational function by approaching the endpoint from within. Thus every nonzero polynomial multiple in these five cases has cofinite support. The argument is about formal identities, leaving specialization at a single number, division by a generator and infinite combinations open. The polylogarithm duplication identity splits by $v_2(m)$, whereas fixing the odd part gives the horizontal rays. [Cohen on Apéry's construction](https://www.numdam.org/article/BSMF_1981__109__269_0.pdf)

## The BBP and Bellard denominators, F5

**Two original BBP terms.** For $p\equiv3,7\bmod 8$, the raw denominator $8k+6=2p$ gives $4k=p-3$. The term is $-2^{-(p-3)}/(2p)$. The common slot $8k+4=4p$ gives $-2^{-2(p-1)}/(2p)$. Clearing both denominators multiplies their sum by $p2^{2p-1}$ and gives $-(2^{p+1}+1)\equiv-5\bmod p$. For $p\equiv1,5\bmod 8$, the same scaling gives $7\bmod p$ by Euler's criterion. In the respective residue classes these integers never vanish. At $p=3$ the literal terms are $-1/6$ and $-1/96$, with sum $-17/96$. The original script inserted the same wrong exponent $p-2$ as the text and asserted `[3]`. The repaired parent test enumerates the four literal slots and passes. A subset of the original signed terms containing a $p$-slot needs another **odd denominator part** divisible by $p$, at least $3p$, to remove that pole. This is a bound on a denominator part. [Bailey, Borwein and Plouffe, formula (1.2)](https://doi.org/10.1090/S0025-5718-97-00856-9)

**Why the BBP rate is $3/2$.** Take the literal unshifted prefix through $k=M$ inclusive and $N=4M$. Multiplication by $2^N$ is a unit modulo each odd prime. Fix $\varepsilon>0$ and $p>\varepsilon N$. For large $N$ no denominator contains $p^2$. Write each odd denominator part divisible by $p$ as $pj$. Multiply the prefix by $p$. With $\chi=(2/p)$ the contributing terms have these rational representatives modulo $p$, whenever the indicated slot occurs:

- $pj=8k+1$ or $8k+5$ gives respectively $+4\chi\,2^{-(j-1)/2}/j$ or $-4\chi\,2^{-(j-1)/2}/j$.
- $pj=4k+3$ gives $-4\,2^{-j}/j$.
- $pj=2k+1$ gives $-2\,2^{-2j}/j$.

Here $j$ is odd. The formulas follow by removing multiples of $p-1$ from the exponent, retaining the quadratic character for half-powers. When $p\equiv1\bmod4$ and $p\le2N$, the first line at $j=1$ is $+4$. All further terms from that line together have absolute sum at most $4/3$, using $1/j\le1/3$ for $j\ge3$. The second line starts at $j\ge3$ and contributes at most $2/9$ in absolute value. The third contributes at most $8/15$. When $p\equiv3\bmod4$ and $p\le N$, the second line at $j=1$ is $-2$. The first starts at $j\ge3$ with absolute sum at most $4/3$, and both other lines have negative sign. Thus the rational sum is nonzero in each claimed range. At fixed $\varepsilon$ there are finitely many patterns and rational sums since $j$ is bounded. Beyond their finitely many numerator primes they survive reduction modulo $p$.

Conversely, primes $1\bmod4$ are at most $2N+O(1)$, and primes $3\bmod4$ are at most $N+O(1)$: in the first line such a prime needs $j\ge3$. Higher prime powers contribute $o(N)$ by the lcm bound. The shifted prefix has at most one residual factor of 2 from the two even slots. Apply the PNT in progressions and let $\varepsilon\downarrow0$. The two classes contribute $N$ and $N/2$ to leading order. This supplies the signed noncancellation step missing from the slot count.

**Why the Bellard rate is 1.** Use $N=10M$ and omit the fixed factor $1/64$. For the five $10k+a=pj$ slots, $a=1,3,5,7,9$, let $c_a=256,-64,-4,-4,1$. After multiplying by $p$ the representative is $(-1)^k c_a2^a2^{-j}/j$. For $p\ne 2,5$ and $p\le N$ the $j=1$ term has absolute value 256. All other terms in these five slots together contribute at most $512\sum_{j\ge3,\ j\ \mathrm{odd}}2^{-j}/3=512/18$. In the two $4k+a=pj$ slots the absolute values of the rational representatives are $32\,2^{-5(j-1)/2}/j$ for either $a=1,3$. This follows from Euler's criterion as above and the respective coefficients $-32,-1$. Their sum of absolute values is at most $32/(1-2^{-5})$. Both bounds together are less than 256. The same finite-pattern argument now gives survival for $\varepsilon N<p\le N$ for large $N$. All denominators divide $64L_{N+9}$ after shifting. The PNT gives the limit 1. The assertion that *every* such prime always survives is false at $M=1,p=7$, where the rational representative equals $-224$. The displayed finite Bellard rates near 1 in the note measure the **odd** part. The full rates at $N=120,240,480$ are $1.02999,1.01620,1.01492$. Independently reducing the literal rational prefixes for $1\le M\le100$ verified the representatives for both formulas, including 13,774 prime/prefix cases in the survival ranges with $p^2$ above the largest raw denominator. [Bellard's original formulas (1) and (2)](https://bellard.org/pi/pi_bin/pi_bin.html)

## Chebyshev bounds and the other classical targets

**Even Chebyshev poles.** Write $H_D(y)=T_{2D}(\sqrt y)$. This is the integer polynomial $T_D(2y-1)$. At $y=-a^2$ its absolute value is $(\rho_a^{2D}+\rho_a^{-2D})/2$ with $\rho_a=a+\sqrt{a^2+1}$. The division by $y+a^2$ is exact after subtracting $H_D(-a^2)$. For any fixed Machin identity the signed sum of the integrals in F5.1 is therefore $\pi+\int_0^1R_{\rm tot}(x^2)\,dx$. With $e,w,Q_D,N$ as specified, nearest-integer rounding gives the explicit estimate
$$
|2^N\pi-Y_N|\le 2^{-w}\left(\sum_i |c_i|\arctan(1/a_i)+\tfrac12\sum_{k=0}^{D-1}(2k+1)^{-1}\right).
$$
The right side is $O(1/D)=O(1/N)$ for fixed poles. The total denominator divides $2^w\operatorname{lcm}(1,3,\ldots,2D-1)$. Its logarithm is at most $2D+o(D)$, since removing powers of 2 from $L_{2D}$ costs only $O(\log D)$. The shift is $2D\log_2\rho_{\min}-O(\log D)$. This proves the stated **limsup upper bound along the specified shifts**. If a bound at every sufficiently large integer shift is desired, successive $N(D)$ have bounded gaps for each fixed family. Divide the next approximant by the corresponding bounded power of 2.

- For Machin the bound is $\log2/\operatorname{asinh}(5)=0.29974731355\ldots$. For Størmer it is $\log2/\operatorname{asinh}(8)=0.24965031539\ldots$. Use the exact expression, “approximately 0.24965”, or the upper bound $0.249651$. The decimal inequality $\le0.24965$ does not follow from the exact upper bound. The table's “Limit rate” column should read “limsup upper bound”.
- The missing family argument is elementary. For every $a\ge1$, $\arctan(1/a)=\arctan(1/(a+1))+\arctan(1/(a^2+a+1))$ by the tangent addition formula with both angles in the principal range. Start with $\pi=4\arctan(1)$ and replace all smallest poles successively. Each finite identity has integer coefficients and the smallest $a$ tends to infinity across identities. Thus for every $\eta>0$ some **fixed finite family** has limsup bound below $\eta$. This order of quantifiers supplies no exact zero rate for one fixed Machin identity.
- I regenerated the coefficients via the recurrence for $T_D(2y-1)$, divided and rounded in rational arithmetic, and checked all nine denominator divisibilities. The two alternating partial sums of lengths 260 and 261 at $1/5$ and $1/239$ bracket the arctangents in the Machin formula. They give rational upper and lower bounds for each error, certify $0<|2^N\pi-Y_N|<1/N$ in all nine rows, and reproduce all the printed error logarithms to two places. For $D=120$ the three shifts are $292,787,948$, with total rates $0.82554,0.29730,0.25398$. The parent script itself uses Decimal and floating point for the error column. Its polynomial arithmetic is exact. The construction establishes neither a homogeneous relation between consecutive numerators nor a digit-frequency result.

**Factorials and $e$.** On multiplying a subset sum by $M!$, its largest index contributes 1. For an odd $p\mid M$, all smaller terms vanish modulo $p$. When $M=2^r\ge4$ choose an odd $p\mid M-1$. Only $M-1$ can contribute in addition to $M$, and the numerator is $1$ or $1+M\equiv2\bmod p$. In each case an odd denominator survives. This also covers subsets involving $0!,1!$ as separate indices. Using common integer denominator $18!$ and subset sums, I found zero dyadic cases in the 524,280 subsets of $\{0,\ldots,18\}$ with maximum at least 3. The parent's 65,528-mask check and the independent count both agree with F5.2. The Taylor rate for $e$ is stated on a density-one set of truncation indices. In the Padé comparison the natural precision is $N_{\rm eff}=-\log_2|e-C_m/B_m|$. The denominators of the diagonal approximants are odd before reduction. In the factorial formula the $j=1$ contribution has magnitude $m(m+1)$, and every $j\ge2$ contribution contains consecutive factors $m+1,m+2$. The factorial asymptotics give $\log |B_m|=m\log m+O(m)$ and $N_{\rm eff}\log2=2m\log m+O(m)$. The upper bound and $\mu(e)=2$ give the limiting reduced rate $(\log2)/2$. Attribute $\mu(e)=2$ to the continued fraction of $e$. Roth's theorem applies to algebraic irrationals.

**Polylogarithms.** The proof of F5.3 works for integer $s\ge1$, $b\ge2$. At each fixed band, multiplication by $p^s$ leaves a nonzero rational residue outside a fixed finite exceptional set, and the full shifted denominator divides $L_N^s$. This proves rate $s$ per **base-$b$ shift**. The single-ray expression printed below F5.3 is binary. For $m=p2^j$, $p\nmid2b$, the necessary congruence for removal of $p$ in general radix is
$$
p\mid \sum_{j\in G} b^{2^K-2^j}2^{s(K-j)} .
$$
It suffices modulo $p$ for the delay obstruction. At order $s>1$, eliminating the full pole can require reduction modulo $p^s$. The sum has bit length $O_{b,s}(2^K+K)$ and the subset count still yields the density-one bound. The every-prime logarithmic estimate has the radix-dependent constant. Full-support digit statistics remain open.

**Apéry and Broadhurst.** For the binomial sum the unreduced lcm has logarithm $4M+o(M)$. For $p>\sqrt{2M}$ the binomial contributes at most one carry, and contributes none when $p\mid k$. Thus the lcm has exponent exactly 3 for $\sqrt{2M}<p\le M$ and 1 for $M<p\le2M$ (take $k=M$ in the latter range). Smaller primes contribute $o(M)$ since each local log contribution is $O(\log M)$ by the carry bound. At $N=2M+O(1)$ the resulting rate 2 is an upper bound for the reduced sum. For the recurrence, the separate upper bound is $(3+4\log(1+\sqrt2))\log2/(8\log(1+\sqrt2))=0.64148848\ldots$ at effective precision. These are distinct approximants. Broadhurst's equations (17) and (48) give a combination of $S_{3,1}$ and $S_{3,3}$ with period-eight coefficient vectors. Grouping three successive periods of the first and one of the second gives radix $2^{12}$ and length 24 after rescaling indices in the second. Presence of a denominator before reduction needs a survival argument. In the band $N+O(1)<p<2N-O(1)$ only $k=p$ in the $S_{3,1}$ sum can contribute a $p$-pole. Its odd-index coefficient is $\pm1$ before the fixed prefactor, and $S_{3,3}$ stops at $2N/3+O(1)$. This proves exponential growth (at least $3N+o(N)$ in log denominator for these truncations). A claim that *all* primes below $2N$ survive does not follow. The script has no Broadhurst computation. [Cohen](https://www.numdam.org/article/BSMF_1981__109__269_0.pdf) · [Broadhurst, equations (17) and (48)](https://arxiv.org/html/math/9803067v1)

**Algebraic examples and discrepancy.** The three Catalan identities check by substituting into the binomial series. The effective denominator exponent of term $k$ is $hk-s_2(k)$ with stride $h=4$ or $5$ and binary digit sum $s_2$. It increases strictly, since $s_2(k+1)-s_2(k)\le1$. Hence the last term gives the claimed excess for $K=\lfloor N/(h-2)\rfloor$. The first example is for $3\sqrt2/4$; division by 3 introduces at most one fixed odd factor for $\sqrt2$. For Pell convergents indexed by $(1+\sqrt2)^m=P_m+Q_m\sqrt2$, **$v_2(Q_m)=v_2(m)$**, unbounded. Indeed $P_m$ is odd, $Q_m$ has the parity of $m$, and $Q_{2m}=2P_mQ_m$. The correct $O(\log m)$ costs $o(N_{\rm eff})$, so the claimed limiting odd rate survives. If index 0 denotes $1/1$, replace $m$ by $m+1$. Primitive-divisor citations for Lucas sequences should state the index convention and exceptional indices (for Pell, $Q_6=70$ has new prime 7 and $Q_{12}=13860$ has new prime 11). The $\mu(\pi)$ consequence in the comparative table is an eventual inequality for **$D_N$**, with gap $c_b\log N/N$, not a strict bound on $D_\infty$. The named algebraic numbers also have unconditional sublinear digit-count bounds.
