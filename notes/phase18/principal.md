# A normality switch for valuation-weighted sparse sums

Fix a radix $b\ge2$, an odd prime $p\nmid b$, and $t=\operatorname{ord}_p(b)$. For integers $s\ge0$, $d\ge2$, put

$$
C(b,p,s,d)=\sum_{k\ge1}\frac{b^{-tk^d}}{p^{s v_p(k)}}.
$$

The extreme discrepancy $D_N(x;b)$ is the supremum over $0\le u<v\le1$ of the absolute difference between $v-u$ and the fraction of $0\le n<N$ with $\{b^nx\}\in[u,v)$. Circular intervals give the same supremum by complementation. At **every** sequence of prefix lengths tending to infinity,

$$
D_N(C(b,p,s,d);b)\longrightarrow
\begin{cases}1 & s=0,\\ 0 & s\ge1.\end{cases}
$$

For $d>s+1$ and $s\ge1$ we prove the complete-period estimate

$$
D_N\ll_{b,p,s,d} N^{-s/d}+N^{-(d-s-1)/d}
       +(1+\log N)N^{-(1-1/d)}.
$$

In particular the binary sum with $(p,s,d)=(3,1,3)$ has $D_N=O(N^{-1/3})$. The argument works for every subgroup generated by $b$. The factorial schedule gives normal Liouville numbers and even admits all nonconstant rational polynomial images. These are paper deductions. The repository supplies the kernel-verified analytic input below. ## From a dense series to a sparse series

Write $H_b(h)=1+b+\cdots+b^{h-1}$. For $m\ge0$, $h\ge1$ and every rational $c$,

$$
\sum_{j=1}^h\frac{c}{H_b(h)b^{m+j}}
=\frac{c}{H_b(h)b^{m+h}}\sum_{j=1}^h b^{h-j}
=c b^{-(m+h)}.
$$

Take consecutive endpoints $0=n_0<n_1<\cdots$ and $h_k=n_k-n_{k-1}$. The raw absolute sum in group $k$ is exactly $|c_k|b^{-n_k}$, so absolute convergence of the grouped series is necessary and sufficient for absolute convergence of the raw series, including signed coefficients. The ordinary raw partial sums then converge to the same limit, including cuts through a group. If every $c_k\ne0$, the raw schedule occupies every positive position. The grouped support has zero density precisely when $K(N)=\#\{k:n_k\le N\}=o(N)$. This is an equality of real numbers. A raw partial group can still have a different rational prefix at that shift.

For $c=A/B$ in lowest terms, factor $B=B_b B_0$, with all primes of $B_b$ dividing $b$ and $(b,B_0)=1$. Once $B_b\mid b^j$, the term completed at $m+h$ has reduced denominator exactly $B_0$ at shift $m+h+j$. Thus the geometric cancellation is exact immediately, and the reduced value becomes integral eventually precisely when $B_0=1$. One can retain $B_0=p^e$ deliberately. A small shifted future tail is a real approximation with a separate estimate below.

**Infinitely many raw denominator primes.** For $c_k=1/p^{s v_p(k)}$ each individual denominator $p^{s v_p(k)} H_b(h_k)b^{n_{k-1}+j}$ is already reduced. If the lengths are unbounded then even the $H_b(h_k)$ have infinitely many distinct prime divisors. For a fixed odd prime $\ell\nmid b$ dividing $b^h-1$, its order $u$ divides $h$, and lifting the exponent gives $v_\ell(b^h-1)=v_\ell(b^u-1)+v_\ell(h/u)$. For $\ell=2$ and odd $b$, the valuation is $v_2(b-1)$ for odd $h$, and $v_2(b^2-1)+v_2(h)-1$ for even $h$. Primes dividing $b$ never divide $H_b(h)$. Thus the entire contribution of any fixed finite set of primes to $H_b(h)$ is polynomial in $h$, whereas $H_b(h)\ge b^{h-1}$. This also proves infinite raw support for reduced $c_k=A_k/B_k\ne0$ along any sequence $h_k\to\infty$ with $\log |A_k|=o(h_k)$, since cancellation removes a divisor of $|A_k|$. Bounded lengths and fixed finite coefficient support give finite raw support. With unrestricted numerators even $c_k=H_b(h_k)$ cancels the whole geometric denominator.

## Exact reduced denominators

Allow any strictly increasing positive $n_k$ with $t\mid n_k$. Put $w(k)=p^{-s v_p(k)}$, $U_k=\sum_{i=1}^k w(i)b^{-n_i}$, $U_0=0$, and $R_n=b^n U_{K(n)}$. For $K(n)=0$ the denominator is one. For $K=K(n)\ge1$ let $J=\lfloor\log_p K\rfloor$. Then

$$
\operatorname{den}(R_n)=p^{sJ},\qquad
\operatorname{den}(U_K)=b^{n_K}p^{sJ}.
$$

For $s=0$ the shifted prefix is integral. For $s\ge1$ the only summands of $p^{sJ}R_n$ surviving modulo $p$ have indices $mp^J$, $1\le m\le\lfloor K/p^J\rfloor<p$. Each contributes $b^n\bmod p$ since $b^{n_i}\equiv1\bmod p$. Their count is between 1 and $p-1$, including at a power of $p$ and just before the next power. This also works for $J=0$. At $n=n_K$, every earlier term of the cleared numerator is divisible by $b$ and the newest term is a power of $p$, coprime to $b$. This gives the unshifted formula even at $s=0$. The insertion at $n_k$ belongs to the new prefix, and all shifts before $n_{k+1}$ have the same denominator. For $n_k=tk^d$, $K=\lfloor(n/t)^{1/d}\rfloor$, so $q_n\asymp n^{s/d}$ at late shifts.

## A fixed-support criterion for all prefixes

Here is the form used also for polynomial images. Let $x=\sum_{m\ge0}a_m b^{-m}$, $a_m\in\mathbb Q$, $|a_m|\le C(m+1)^A$ for fixed $C,A\ge0$. Denote by $I(N)$ the number of nonzero positions through $N$. Form the exact shifted prefix $R_n$ by retaining $m\le n$. Assume

1. $I(N)=O(N^{1-\eta})$ for some $0<\eta\le1$.
2. For fixed integers $c\ge2$, $(c,b)=1$, and some constant $D>0$, each $q_n=\operatorname{den}(R_n)$ divides a power of $c$ and $q_n\ll(n+1)^D$.
3. For each fixed $M$, $\#\{n<N:q_n<M\}=o(N)$.

Then $x$ is base-$b$ normal. The kernel theorem used here states that for each integer $d_0\ge1$ and $\epsilon>0$, the sum of $\exp(2\pi i b^j y)$ for $0\le j<T$ has modulus at most $\epsilon T$ whenever the reduced denominator $q$ of $y$ exceeds a threshold depending only on $b,c,d_0,\epsilon$, divides a power of $c$, and satisfies $q\le T^{d_0}$. [Definition and proof](../../NormalityChecker/SmoothOrbit.lean#L16) · [Paper I, section on denominator survival](../../papers/01-stoneham-polynomials/paper.tex#L244)

**Proof.** Fix frequency $h\ne0$ and partition $[0,N)$ into blocks of length $T=\lfloor N^{\eta/2}\rfloor$. Charge the last partial block and every block containing an insertion, in total at most $T(1+I(N))=o(N)$. Within each remaining block the rational model is $b^jR_a$ from its start $a$, with constant denominator since $(b,c)=1$. Blocks below the required denominator floor times $|h|$ cost $o(N)$ by hypothesis 3. The reduced denominator of $hR_a$ lies between $q_a/|h|$ and $q_a$. It still divides a power of $c$. Choose an integer $d_0>2D/\eta$ so $q_a\le T^{d_0}$ eventually, uniformly in $a<N$. This checks all hypotheses of the repository theorem.

To compare real orbits, set $H=\lceil(A+3)\log_b(2N)\rceil$. The shifts with an insertion in $(n,n+H]$ number at most $H I(N+H)=o(N)$. At all other shifts

$$
|b^nx-R_n|\le C\sum_{j>H}(N+j+1)^A b^{-j}
\ll_{A,b,C}(N+H+1)^A b^{-H}=o(1).
$$

The bound follows by writing $j=H+u$ and summing $(1+u)^A b^{-u}$. Consequently each fixed-frequency real mean differs from the model mean by $o(1)$. The model mean has limsup in modulus at most $\epsilon$ for arbitrary $\epsilon>0$. Weyl's criterion gives uniform distribution and hence vanishing extreme discrepancy, by bracketing intervals with a fixed fine grid. The argument takes an arbitrary $N$.

For the principal family use $I(N)\ll N^{1/d}$, $c=p$, bounded weights, and the exact denominator above. This proves the positive-order switch also when the period is longer than a gap. At $s=0$ every $R_n$ is integral. At fixed $H$, at most $H I(N+H)$ shifts have a nearby next insertion, and elsewhere the positive tail is at most $b^{-H}/(b-1)$. For any $\epsilon>0$ choose $H$ making this less than $\epsilon$. The interval $[0,\epsilon)$ has frequency tending to one and gives $\liminf D_N\ge1-\epsilon$. This last argument needs only $I(N)=o(N)$.

## Complete periods and the unfinished gap

Set $a=v_p(b^t-1)$. For $e\ge a$, $\operatorname{ord}_{p^e}(b)=t p^{e-a}$ by lifting the exponent. The subgroup generated by $b$ projects modulo $p^a$ onto $t$ residues and is their **full inverse image** among units, by the order formula. For any reduced numerator, the complete orbit is therefore a union of $t$ equally weighted grids of mesh $p^{a-e}$. The fraction of any one grid in a half-open interval differs from its length by at most $p^{a-e}$. Thus one full period has unnormalized error at most $t$ when $e\ge a$. For $e<a$ the period has at most $t$ points, and $p^a/q\ge1$ also covers denominator one. With $q=p^e$, any consecutive $L$ steps of the rational orbit have unnormalized interval error at most

$$
p^a L/q+q.
$$

Split into full periods and one remainder shorter than the order, which is at most $q$. This estimate includes arbitrarily small $L$.

Apply it on $[n_k,\min(n_{k+1},N))$ with length $L_k$, $q_k=p^{s\lfloor\log_p k\rfloor}\asymp k^s$. Initial shifts cost $O(1)$. For $K=K(N)$, $L_k\ll k^{d-1}$ and $N\asymp K^d$ at large $N$. If $d>s+1$,

$$
\sum_{k\le K} L_k/q_k\ll K^{d-s},\qquad
\sum_{k\le K}q_k\ll K^{s+1}.
$$

Use $H=\lceil 2\log_b(2N)\rceil$. At most $H I(N+H)\ll N^{1/d}\log(2N)$ positions have a nearby insertion, and at the others the tail is $O(N^{-2})$. Enlarging or shrinking each test interval on the circle costs at most twice this radius in length, plus the exceptional frequency in the counts. Dividing the two sums by $N$ proves the opening estimate. For $(b,p,s,d)=(2,3,1,3)$, $t=2$, $a=1$, and the subgroup is the full unit group. For fixed $d$ the best exponent in this **complete-period bound** is $\lfloor(d-1)/2\rfloor/d$ when admissible. At fixed $s$ it is best at $d=2s+1$, giving $s/(2s+1)$, which approaches $1/2$.

**A sharper incomplete-period bound.** On the last fragment of a period, of length $R\le q$, the unnormalized interval error is $O_p(\sqrt q\log^2(2q))$. Here are details. Write $e_q(z)=\exp(2\pi i z/q)$. On the cyclic time group of order $T=\operatorname{ord}_q(b)$ the indicator of the fragment has normalized Fourier coefficients with sum of absolute values $O(\log(2T))$, by summing the elementary geometric-series bound $\min(1,1/(2\min(j,T-j)))$ for $0<j<T$. Every character of $\langle b\rangle$ extends to the finite abelian unit group. Averaging its extensions expresses each time-character sum of $e_q(ha b^i)$, with $(a,q)=1$, as an average of Gauss sums over units. For $g=(h,q)$ each Gauss sum has absolute value at most $\sqrt{qg}$. Indeed projection to units modulo $q/g$ cancels unless the character is constant on the kernel. In the latter case the fibre size is at most $g$. At a unit additive frequency modulo $q/g$, all such Fourier coefficients of a multiplicative character have equal modulus and Plancherel bounds this by $\sqrt{q/g}$. Modulus one also satisfies the bound. Thus every fragment exponential sum is $O(\sqrt{qg}\log(2q))$. In Erdős–Turán with cutoff $q$, the length term is $O(1)$ and

$$
\sum_{h\le q}\frac{\sqrt{(h,q)}}h
\le (1+\log q)\sum_{j\ge0}p^{-j/2}.
$$

This proves the fragment estimate, also for a translated starting numerator. Replacing $\sum q_k$ above by $\sum\sqrt{q_k}\log^2(2q_k)$ gives a term $O(N^{-(d-1-s/2)/d}\log^2(2N))$. For $s\ge d$ the mesh sum divided by $N$ is $O(\log(2N)/N)$, absorbed by the insertion cost. Hence this method saves a power whenever $d>1+s/2$. Balancing the two powers gives $d=1+3s/2$ for even $s$, with exponents approaching $2/3$ up to logarithms. These are optimizations of upper estimates.

**Comparison with shorter orbits.** The internal Lean theorem supplies qualitative cancellation. The following power saving for every fixed $s\ge1$, $d\ge2$ is a paper deduction from Vandehey's quantitative Corollary 1.2. For a fixed finite prime set $S$, $m$ supported on $S$, $(m,b)=1$, $b\ge2$, $(z,m)=1$, and $\epsilon>0$, that result gives $\delta=\delta(\epsilon)>0$ and $C=C(\epsilon,b,S)$ such that for every $T\ge m^\epsilon$,

$$
\left|\sum_{j=1}^{T}\exp(2\pi i z b^j/m)\right|
\le C T m^{-\delta}(1+\log m).
$$

It is uniform in the reduced numerator. A block starting with index zero uses $z b^{-1}\bmod m$ in this formula. [Vandehey, Corollary 1.2](https://arxiv.org/html/1606.07911#S1.Thmthm2)

On an arbitrary prefix of $[X,2X)$ take blocks of length $\lfloor X^\beta\rfloor$, $0<\beta<1-1/d$. Insertion and unfinished blocks cost $O(X^{1/d+\beta}+X^\beta)$. On good blocks $q\asymp X^{s/d}$. For $h\le X^{s/(2d)}$ the reduced modulus $q'$ satisfies $X^{s/(2d)}\ll q'\ll X^{s/d}$ and has support $\{p\}$. Here $S=\{p\}$, $(q',b)=1$, and reduction at frequency $h$ produces a unit numerator. The block length is $\gg(q')^{\beta d/s}$, so it exceeds $(q')^\epsilon$ for $\epsilon=\beta d/(2s)$ eventually. The corollary bounds each normalized sum by $O(X^{-\delta s/(2d)}\log(2X))$. Erdős–Turán adds a harmonic logarithm. Compare the interval counts to the real orbit by the same $H=O(\log X)$ buffer, and sum the dyadic bounds, including the last partial shell. The resulting exponent depends on the short-sum input. [Vandehey, preprint Corollary 1.2](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1606.07911) · [Repository quantitative comparison](../phase13/quantitative-normality.md)

## Other schedules, irrationality and transcendence

For any increasing positive multiples $n_k$ of $t$, the same switch holds if $K(N)\ll N^{1-\eta}$ for some $\eta>0$. Indeed $q_n\le K(n)^s\le(n+1)^s$ and, for $s>0$, $q_n\to\infty$ however slowly $K(n)$ grows. This includes eventually increasing integer polynomial schedules of degree at least two (with the required positive multiples), geometric schedules, factorial schedules and faster or irregular ones with the count bound. There is no upper lacunarity restriction. At $s=0$ zero-density insertions suffice. These are sufficient schedule conditions.

Write $Q_k=b^{n_k}p^{s\lfloor\log_p k\rfloor}$, the exact reduced denominator of $U_k$. Positivity and integer spacing give the two-sided tail estimate

$$
(k+1)^{-s} b^{-n_{k+1}}\le X-U_k\le \frac{b}{b-1} b^{-n_{k+1}}.
$$

Also $\log_b Q_k=n_k+O(\log k)=(1+o(1))n_k$. If $\limsup n_{k+1}/n_k>1$, then along a subsequence $Q_k(X-U_k)\to0$. A rational $X=A/B$ would have $X-U_k\ge1/(BQ_k)$ since the tail is positive. Thus $X$ is irrational by rational separation independently of its digit distribution. If the limsup is infinite, then for every $M>0$ infinitely many of these distinct approximants satisfy $0<X-U_k<Q_k^{-M}$. This proves the Liouville assertion, and hence transcendence. For $n_k=tk!$ starting at $k=1$, the first two endpoints are $t,2t$ and the ratios are $k+1$. For every $s\ge1$ the same number is normal by the schedule theorem.

When $\limsup n_{k+1}/n_k>1$ is finite, choose $1<\mu<\limsup n_{k+1}/n_k$. Along a subsequence $0<X-U_k<Q_k^{-\mu}$. All $Q_k$ are supported on the fixed primes dividing $bp$, and the numerators have size $O(Q_k)$. Ridout's restricted-denominator theorem therefore excludes every algebraic irrational. This transcendence proof uses only exponent $>1$. [Restricted-denominator application and reference in Paper III](../../papers/03-signed-resonance/paper.tex#L311)

In fact for $n_{k+1}/n_k\to g>1$ finite, the irrationality exponent satisfies

$$
g\le\mu(X)\le\max\{g,\,1+g/(g-1)\}.
$$

For the upper bound fix small $\epsilon>0$ with $g-1-\epsilon>0$. For a large denominator $q$ choose the first $k$ with $(g-1-\epsilon)n_k\ge\log_b(2q)$. The tail bound gives $Q_k(X-U_k)\le b^{-(g-1-\epsilon)n_k}\le1/(2q)$ for large $k$. Minimality gives $n_k\le(g+\epsilon)\log_b(2q)/(g-1-\epsilon)$. If a reduced $a/q$ differs from $U_k$, rational separation gives $|X-a/q|\ge1/(2qQ_k)$. If $a/q=U_k$, then $q=Q_k$, and the lower tail bound gives $|X-a/q|\ge q^{-g-o(1)}$. Letting $\epsilon\downarrow0$ proves the upper bound. The upper tail estimate along the truncations gives the lower exponent. In particular geometric schedules with ratio $g$ give transcendental **non-Liouville** examples, with $\mu(X)=g$ for $g\ge(3+\sqrt5)/2$. For $n_k=tk^d$ the ratio tends to one. Transcendence remains open in general, but for $s\ge1$ the polynomial normality theorem excludes algebraic degrees through $\min(s,d-1)$.
