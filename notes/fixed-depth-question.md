# When does one prime pass at a finite depth?

**Two-prime theorem (paper proof).** Fix distinct primes $`p,q`$, a radix $`b\ge2`$ with $`p\nmid b`$, and an order $`s\ge1`$. For the full unsigned support $`\{p,q\}`$, some finite precision of the **selected $`p`$-test** passes on every cell. This holds for every residue of the radix, including when $`q`$ also lies off the radix. For odd $`p`$, every one-sided value of the associated $`p`$-adic moment is transcendental over $`\mathbb Q`$.

**Large-order theorem (paper proof).** Fix the selected prime $`p`$. For all sufficiently large orders $`s`$, the top row alone certifies every finite unsigned support containing $`p`$, uniformly in the radix coprime to $`p`$. The required precision is $`o(s)`$ as $`s`$ grows. At $`p=3`$ the top row suffices for every $`s\ge2`$.

The general existence question remains open here for a fixed small order and two or more cofactor primes. Lean proves nullity of persistent failure and convergence of the finite discrepancy bounds for arbitrary finite support. The finite-termination results above are deductions on paper. ## One-sided moments and the finite test

For a finite prime set $`P`$, the full unsigned expansion sums $`1/(m^s b^m)`$ over every positive integer $`m`$ with prime factors in $`P`$, including 1. Write $`S`$ for the positive integers generated by $`P\setminus\{p\}`$. For odd $`p`$ let $`w`$ be the Teichmüller lift of $`b^{-1}\bmod p`$, so $`w^{p-1}=1`$. Choose either $`\triangleleft`$ equal to $`\le`$ with $`1\le t<p`$, or $`\triangleleft`$ equal to $`<`$ with $`1<t\le p`$. The two choices give the value on the right and the limit from the left, respectively. Put

```math
V(t)=\sum_{i\ge0}p^{si}\sum_{m\in S,\ m\,\triangleleft\,p^it}\frac{w^m}{m^s}.
```

The top row has index 0. Rows with $`si\ge L`$ vanish modulo $`p^L`$. The remaining rows give the checker's step function on the indicated side of each threshold. If every precision has a failing cell, choose a failing point at each successive precision. Compactness gives a subsequence approaching a point from the left, from the right, or constantly. At each fixed precision the mesh is finite and failure is nested, so its residue on that side is zero. Conversely a one-sided zero gives a failing interval of positive length on that side at every precision. Thus excluding both kinds of zero proves finite termination. For $`p=2`$ the top row always contains just 1 and passes modulo 2. ## One cofactor prime and all periodic weights

Here $`S=\{q^j:j\ge0\}`$. Put $`\alpha=\log_p q`$ and $`\beta=\log_p t`$, using real logarithms. The first row admitting $`q^j`$ has index $`k_j=\lceil j\alpha-\beta\rceil`$ on the right, or $`k_j=\lfloor j\alpha-\beta\rfloor+1`$ on the left. In both cases $`k_0=0`$ and $`k_j=j\alpha+O(1)`$. Set $`a_j=w^{q^j}`$ and $`x_j=p^{s k_j}/q^{sj}`$. Summing the geometric series of rows containing each cofactor gives

```math
(1-p^s)V(t)=\sum_{j\ge0}a_j x_j.
```

The rearrangement is exact modulo each $`p^L`$ since only finitely many pairs contribute. The coefficients $`a_j`$ are eventually periodic, even if $`q`$ divides the order of $`w`$. Remove their finite preperiod and shift the starting index to zero. This subtracts an algebraic number and multiplies the tail by a nonzero algebraic factor when we normalize $`k_j`$ by subtracting its new initial value. The shifted $`k_j`$ still has the same ceiling or strict floor rule with a real intercept. It suffices to show that the resulting sum $`T`$ is transcendental. The proof works for any periodic sequence of algebraic coefficients in a fixed $`p`$-adic field, provided at least one residue class has nonzero weight.

**Near returns of the exponents.** Let $`D`$ be a period of $`a_j`$ and take reduced continued-fraction convergents $`A/Q`$ to the irrational number $`\alpha`$. Set $`h=DQ`$, $`u=DA`$ and $`X=p^{su}/q^{sh}`$. Then $`0<|h\alpha-u|<D/Q`$. For all large $`Q`$, the rounded exponents obey $`k_j=k_{j-h}+u`$ for $`j\ge h`$ except when a rotation phase lies in an interval on the circle of length $`|h\alpha-u|`$. This follows by comparing the rounding of two numbers that differ by $`h\alpha-u`$ after removing the integer $`u`$. The interval is half-open for either rounding convention. At an exception the discrepancy of the two integers is $`1`$ or $`-1`$.

For each $`h`$ there are infinitely many exceptions in every residue class modulo $`D`$, by density of the irrational rotation by $`D\alpha`$. Their minimum separation in the index $`j`$ tends to infinity with $`Q`$. Indeed, two exceptions a fixed distance $`r>0`$ apart would require the distance of $`r\alpha`$ to the nearest integer to be at most $`D/Q`$. Also, **for every fixed integer $`B>1`$, the number of exceptions in $`[h,Bh]`$ stays bounded**. In any $`Q`$ consecutive positions the phases are within $`1/Q`$ of a translate of the grid with spacing $`1/Q`$. Use $`|\alpha-A/Q|<1/Q^2`$ for the offsets $`0,\ldots,Q-1`$ and coprimality of $`A,Q`$. An interval of length $`D/Q`$ therefore captures at most $`D+4`$ positions in such a block. At most $`BD+1`$ blocks cover the indicated range.

**The shifted sum.** Write $`E_h`$ for exceptional indices $`j\ge h`$ with $`a_j\ne0`$. Subtract the shifted copy of the series, using $`a_{j-h}=a_j`$, and set

```math
Y_h=(1-X)T-\sum_{j<h}a_j x_j
    =\sum_{j\in E_h}c_{h,j}x_j.
```

Here $`c_{h,j}=a_j(1-p^{s(u+k_{j-h}-k_j)})`$. The exception coefficients lie in a fixed finite set of nonzero algebraic numbers. The increasing gaps imply that for large $`h`$ the first term of the sum on the right has strictly largest $`p`$-adic absolute value. The same holds for every nonempty tail of that sum, since $`k_j=j\alpha+O(1)`$ and the coefficients range over a finite set.

**An application of the Subspace Theorem.** Suppose $`T`$ is algebraic. Take a number field $`K`$ containing $`T`$ and the coefficients, with the chosen place $`v`$ over $`p`$. Use absolute values normalized for the product formula. Choose a finite set of places $`\mathcal S`$ containing the archimedean places, the places over $`p,q`$, and the denominators of the fixed algebraic data. Each $`x_j`$ is an $`\mathcal S`$-unit, so its product of absolute values over $`\mathcal S`$ equals 1. Each $`Y_h`$ is an $`\mathcal S`$-integer. There are constants $`C,c>0`$, independent of $`B`$, with

```math
\prod_{z\in\mathcal S,\ z\ne v}|Y_h|_z\le e^{Ch},
\qquad |x_j|_v\ll e^{-cj}.
```

For the first bound use the finite expression for $`Y_h`$ on the left above. The exponents of $`p`$ and $`q`$ in it are $`O(h)`$, and it has $`h+1`$ terms with fixed algebraic coefficients besides the factor $`1-X`$. The local triangle inequalities give the bound at the finitely many places. The second estimate follows from $`k_j=j\alpha+O(1)`$.

Fix an integer $`B`$ with $`cB>C+1`$. Take an infinite subsequence with a fixed number $`M`$ of exceptions through $`Bh`$ and a fixed ordered list $`c_1,\ldots,c_M`$ of their coefficients. Their positions $`n_1<\cdots<n_M`$ may depend on $`h`$. Truncation has error

```math
\left|Y_h-\sum_{i=1}^{M}c_i x_{n_i}\right|_v\ll e^{-cBh}.
```

If $`M=0`$, this estimate contradicts the product formula for the nonzero $`\mathcal S`$-integer $`Y_h`$. Otherwise apply Schlickewei's Subspace Theorem to the vector $`(Y_h,x_{n_1},\ldots,x_{n_M})`$. At $`v`$ replace its first coordinate form by the linear form in the error estimate. Use coordinate forms elsewhere. The product of all chosen forms over $`\mathcal S`$ is at most a constant times $`e^{(C-cB)h}`$, because every $`x_{n_i}`$ has product 1. The projective height of the vector is at most $`e^{C_B h}`$ for a fixed $`C_B>0`$, by the same local bounds and $`n_i\le Bh`$. Thus the product is at most a fixed negative power of the height for large $`h`$. The theorem puts infinitely many of the vectors in one proper linear subspace of $`K^{M+1}`$. [Schlickewei's theorem in precisely this form, Luca–Ouaknine–Worrell, Theorem 2](https://doi.org/10.1112/blms.70033)

In the resulting fixed linear relation, the coefficient of $`Y_h`$ must be nonzero. Among the $`x_{n_i}`$ with nonzero coefficient, the earliest one would otherwise have strictly largest absolute value at $`v`$ for all large $`h`$, by the increasing gaps. Write the relation as $`Y_h=\sum_i d_i x_{n_i}`$. Subtract it from the infinite exceptional sum. Its coefficients now belong to another fixed finite set. After removing zero terms there is a first nonzero term, since infinitely many exceptions follow $`Bh`$. Its absolute value strictly exceeds that of all later terms for large $`h`$. Their sum cannot vanish. This proves transcendence and excludes both one-sided zeros.

## An elementary bound when the weights are signs

There is also a constructive argument if $`a_j=a\epsilon_j`$ for a fixed unit $`a`$ and signs $`\epsilon_j\in\{-1,1\}`$, for instance whenever $`b\equiv\pm1\pmod p`$. Divide by $`a`$. Write $`J`$ and $`D`$ for a preperiod and period of the signs. Define $`u_n=p^{k_n}t/q^n`$, in $`[1,p)`$ on the right or $`(1,p]`$ on the left. Multiplying the series by $`q^{sn}/p^{sk_n}`$ splits it into a prefix

```math
A_n=\sum_{j<n}\epsilon_j\frac{q^{s(n-j)}}{p^{s(k_n-k_j)}}
```

and a tail

```math
H_n=\sum_{j\ge0}\epsilon_{n+j}\frac{p^{s(k_{n+j}-k_n)}}{q^{sj}}.
```

Here $`k_{n+j}-k_n`$ is the first row admitting $`q^j`$ at $`u_n`$ on the chosen side. Hence $`H_n`$ is the geometric-row series at $`u_n`$ with the shifted signs. The only possible denominator prime of $`A_n`$ is $`p`$. Its ordinary absolute value is at most $`np^s`$, since the magnitude of each summand is $`(u_j/u_n)^s`$. If the original test fails modulo $`p^L`$, then $`A_n+H_n`$ vanishes modulo $`p^{L-sk_n}`$. Whenever $`L\ge sk_n`$, integrality of $`H_n`$ forces $`A_n`$ to be an ordinary integer.

The following choices give a finite depth bound, usually much larger than the first passing depth. Put $`F=1+\lfloor 1/\alpha\rfloor`$. Increase $`N>J`$ until, with $`d=1+\lceil\log_p(2Np^s)\rceil`$,

```math
N-J+1>DF\left(2+\left\lfloor\frac{\lceil d/s\rceil}{\alpha}\right\rfloor\right).
```

Such an $`N`$ exists because the right side grows logarithmically. Put $`\delta=\min_{1\le r\le N}\|r\alpha\|`$, where the norm here is distance to the nearest integer. Choose $`K`$ such that the largest circular gap of the phases $`j\alpha\bmod 1`$, $`1\le j\le K`$, is less than $`\delta`$. Density guarantees this choice. A passing precision is

```math
L=s\lceil N\alpha\rceil+\max\{d,\ s\lceil K\alpha\rceil+1\}.
```

Indeed $`k_n\le\lceil N\alpha\rceil`$ for $`n\le N`$. Partition the logarithmic phase interval into $`F`$ equal pieces of diameter less than $`\alpha`$. For a fixed suffix of signs, $`H_n\bmod p^d`$ as a function of $`u_n`$ has at most $`2+\lfloor\lceil d/s\rceil/\alpha\rfloor`$ values. Its interior thresholds come from $`q^j/p^i`$ with $`si<d`$. Each $`q^j`$ supplies at most one interior threshold and satisfies $`q^j<p^{\lceil d/s\rceil}`$. At most $`D`$ suffixes occur for $`n\ge J`$. By pigeonhole two indices in $`[J,N]`$ have the same suffix, piece and residue. If the test failed, the corresponding integers $`A_n`$ would agree modulo $`p^d`$ and hence be equal, as $`p^d>2Np^s`$.

The two log phases differ by at least $`\delta`$ since their difference modulo 1 is a nonzero multiple $`r\alpha`$ with $`|r|\le N`$. A phase $`j\alpha\bmod1`$ with $`j\le K`$ lies strictly between them. Thus the two row sums for $`H_n`$ differ by row $`\lceil K\alpha\rceil`$ at the latest. At the earliest changed row just one power of $`q`$ crosses, since the endpoint ratio is less than $`q`$. Its unit coefficient gives valuation exactly $`si`$ at that row $`i`$. Later rows have higher valuation and the geometric factor $`1-p^s`$ is a unit. This contradicts equal $`A_n`$, which would force the two tails to agree modulo $`p^{s\lceil K\alpha\rceil+1}`$. Strictly interior separation works for either endpoint convention.

For instance, take $`p=3`$, $`q=2`$, $`s=1`$ and $`w=1`$. The choices $`N=22`$, $`d=6`$ and $`K=149`$ give $`L=110`$. To check the gap comparison exactly, sort the numbers $`2^j/3^{\lfloor j\alpha\rfloor}`$ for $`1\le j\le149`$ in $`[1,3)`$. The largest ratio of circular neighbors, including the wrap multiplied by 3, is $`2^{65}/3^{41}`$, smaller than $`3^\delta=3^{12}/2^{19}`$. The direct checker already passes at precision three.

## Arbitrarily many cofactor primes at large order

No nonempty row of the full unsigned series can cancel exactly as an algebraic number. If its cofactor set contains more than 1, take the smallest cofactor prime $`r`$ and the largest power $`r^e`$ admitted by that row. This is its unique term whose denominator has $`r`$-valuation $`es`$. Any larger cofactor divisible by $`r^e`$ would be at least $`r^{e+1}`$. At any place over $`r`$, the roots of unity are units, so the term with cofactor $`r^e`$ has strictly smallest valuation. The row containing just 1 is a unit as well. This still permits cancellation *between* rows in the $`p`$-adic limit.

The possible top rows at a fixed $`p`$ are among the sums $`U_s=\sum_{m\in A} w^m m^{-s}`$ with $`1\in A\subseteq\{1,\ldots,p-1\}`$. Fix $`A,w`$ temporarily. For every $`\varepsilon>0`$, only finitely many $`s`$ satisfy $`|U_s|_p\le p^{-\varepsilon s}`$. When $`A=\{1\}`$ this is immediate. Otherwise apply the same Subspace Theorem over $`\mathbb Q`$ to the vector $`(m^{-s})_{m\in A}`$ at the place $`p`$, taking the sum with the algebraic coefficients $`w^m`$ as the linear form replacing the coordinate for $`m=1`$. All coordinates are units outside the finite set consisting of infinity and primes at most $`p`$. The omitted coordinate is 1, so the product in the theorem is exactly $`|U_s|_p`$. The projective height is at most $`\mathrm{lcm}(A)^s`$, by clearing denominators. The proposed inequality gives a fixed negative power of that height. Each resulting proper rational subspace contains only finitely many of the vectors. In a nonzero rational linear relation among the $`m^{-s}`$, the term of smallest $`m`$ with nonzero coefficient dominates in the ordinary real absolute value as $`s\to\infty`$.

There are finitely many $`A,w`$ at fixed $`p`$. Consequently the maximum of the top-row valuations is $`o(s)`$ for large $`s`$. One more than that maximum is eventually at most $`s`$, so every later row is zero at the required precision and every cell passes. This proof gives no numerical order threshold for a general $`p`$.

For $`p=3`$ the only extra top-row cofactor possible is 2. When it occurs, the valuation is that of $`2^s+w`$, with $`w=\pm1`$. If 3 divides this integer, apply lifting the exponent to $`2^s+1`$ for odd $`s`$ or to $`4^{s/2}-1`$ for even $`s`$. The resulting valuation is $`1+v_3(s)`$. Otherwise the valuation is zero. For $`s\ge2`$ the bound $`1+v_3(s)<s`$ proves the stated uniform result. At $`s=1`$, persistent finite failure would require $`w=1`$ and cofactor 2. It also requires another cofactor prime below 27. Without one, the first three levels are identical to those of $`\{2,3\}`$, because their row cutoffs are strictly below 27 on the right. At precision three the residues on successive cells are $`19,13,22,9,6`$, all nonzero modulo 27.

## A single fixed mask with failure at every depth

At $`b=10`$, $`p=3`$, $`s=1`$ and $`P=\{2,3,5\}`$, the unmasked test passes at precision two. With the same data there is a single unsigned **cofactor mask** that fails at every precision on a neighborhood of $`t=5/2`$ whose width may depend on the precision. Retain every $`3^k m`$ for each chosen cofactor $`m`$, for all $`k\ge0`$. All other terms are omitted. [Full-support table modulo 9](localized-certificates.md#the-five-decimal-residues)

Here $`w=1`$. If cofactor $`m`$ first enters at row $`i`$, its entire contribution to $`(1-3)V(5/2)`$ is $`3^i/m`$. Start by choosing 1 and 2, giving $`U_0=3/2`$ divisible by 3 in $`\mathbb Z_3`$. At step $`i\ge1`$ put $`y=(5/2)3^{i-1}`$. Use as candidates the least $`2^a>y`$ and the least $`5\cdot2^b>y`$, with $`a,b\ge0`$. They are distinct and both lie in $`(y,3y)`$, including at the first step where they are 4 and 5. Each can therefore adjust the new digit without affecting any earlier digit.

Inductively the accumulated rational $`U_{i-1}`$ is divisible by $`3^i`$. Choose the smallest-cardinality candidate subset $`C_i`$ whose reciprocal residues sum to $`-U_{i-1}/3^i\bmod3`$, breaking ties by the displayed candidate order. The subset sums of two nonzero residues cover $`\mathbb F_3`$, so the rule always succeeds. Set

```math
U_i=U_{i-1}+3^i\sum_{m\in C_i}\frac1m .
```

Then $`3^{i+1}`$ divides $`U_i`$ in $`\mathbb Z_3`$. The union of these choices defines one mask for all depths. The geometric-row series is the $`3`$-adic limit of the $`U_i`$, hence zero. Every finite cutoff $`3^i t`$ is a half-integer, so the failure extends to both sides at each finite level. Infinitely many cofactors are retained because a finite positive rational sum $`U_i`$ cannot be divisible by arbitrarily large powers of 3. All cofactors divisible by 25 are omitted. The first six steps illustrate the deterministic rule.

| Row | Candidates | Required residue mod 3 | Retain |
| ---: | --- | ---: | --- |
| 1 | 4, 5 | 1 | 4 |
| 2 | 8, 10 | 2 | 8 |
| 3 | 32, 40 | 1 | 40 |
| 4 | 128, 80 | 1 | 128, 80 |
| 5 | 256, 320 | 0 | None |
| 6 | 1024, 640 | 1 | 1024 |

With several cofactor primes, shifting one exponent also leaves an infinite sum over the other exponents in the first $`h`$ slices. This leaves an infinite boundary contribution in the formula for $`Y_h`$, whose bounded height in the proof came from a finite prefix. The known depth-12 record uses a single fixed support and terminates. The construction reaching an arbitrarily prescribed depth chooses new support primes as the depth grows.
