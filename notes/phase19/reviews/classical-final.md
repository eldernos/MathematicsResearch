# Final audit of the positive logarithm groups and denominator loss

**Verdict.** The earliest completion index for one prescribed prime and the mean loss bound with exponent $3/7$ both follow from the proofs. The Phase 19 survey and registry entries OP-19 and OP-20 correctly present them as paper results. The order-two extension in Corollary 5.4 needs a prime restriction and a qualification about partial survival. The survey statistics also use two different conventions at 3. This audit covers the author's Track E commit `8e78c37` and the new registry and survey text in the integration checkout. ## Corrections to the author's statements

1. **Dilogarithm counterexample.** Corollary 5.4 states the logarithm criterion for odd primes, then extends it to the dilogarithm. The extension requires $p\ge5$. At $p=3$, the top block is $1/2+1/16=9/16$ modulo 3. At $n=6$, $a=1$, the shifted dilogarithm prefix reduces to $5587/150$. Its denominator contains exactly one factor of 3, although 3 is an ordinary base-2 non-Wieferich prime. For $p\ge5$, Granville's congruence detects survival of the **full** power $p^{2a}$. Even at $a=1$, a Wieferich prime can survive once in the dilogarithm. At $p=1093$, $n=1092p$, scaling this prefix by $p^2$ gives residue $713729=653p\pmod{p^2}$. Only for the order-one logarithm does losing the full power at $a=1$ imply disappearance. State $a\ge1$ throughout and retain $p\nmid b(b-1)$ for the rational Fermat quotient in the general-base corollary. [Granville, §2](https://www.kurims.kyoto-u.ac.jp/EMIS/journals/INTEGERS/papers/e22/e22.pdf)
2. **Two minima at 3.** In §1 the allowed multipliers are $1\le j<p$, so $k(3)=k^*(3)=\infty$. The two available residues are both 2. The script's unrestricted search skips multiples of 3 and returns 4 for both minima, with witness $\{1,4\}$. Under the definition in §1, the sums over $5\le p\le100,1000,10000$ are respectively 147, 1476 and 15086. Adding 4 recovers all three printed sums in §7 and in the earlier review. Restrict the finite-excess claim to $p\ge5$ or explicitly label the extended statistic. The separate global statement $M_{\rm glob}(3)=12$ is correct.
3. **Odd-block proof and upper bound.** In Theorem 5.2 take half the **difference** of the evaluations at $1/2$ and $-1/2$ to obtain the odd block. This gives the stated $3q_p(3)/4$. In the introduction copy the ceiling from Theorem 3.3 if citing that proof. Theorem 3.4 needs $2\le K<p$ for its displayed residues. Its Fourier condition suffices for hitting the one target $-x_1$. No necessity of full subset-sum equidistribution follows from this criterion.
4. **Model comparison.** Specify the cutoff for the final comparison of $k$ and $k^*$. On $100<p\le200000$, they agree for 10575 of 17959 primes and the largest gap is 10. On $100<p\le10^6$, they agree for 45842 of 78473 and the largest gap is 11. The 15-cell statistic 31.0 compares counts with three simulated samples of nonzero residues. It is descriptive. The seeded lower-tail mean is $65/3$, against three observed primes. The claimed shape and a model-based conjecture are distinct from a proved logarithmic upper bound.

## Proof of the two registered conclusions

**OP-19.** Write $a_m=1/(m2^m)$ and $x_j=(j2^j)^{-1}\bmod p$. Below $p^2$, clearing a factor $p$ leaves just the terms $x_j$ for indices $pj$ in the group. Thus any group containing $a_p$ and closing before $p\,k(p)$ would give a smaller local witness. For $p\ge5$, the four subset sums of $x_2,x_3$ are distinct because $x_2/x_3=3\ne\pm1$. Each additional nonzero residue increases the size of a proper sumset under translation. The $p-4$ remaining residues therefore suffice to attain $-x_1$ and $k(p)<p$.

Take any local witness with maximum $k<p$. Process the remaining odd primes $q\le k$ in descending order. At valuation $-e$, the $q-1$ candidates $a_{q^et}$, $1\le t<q$, have nonzero scaled residues whose subset sums fill the field. Clearing that digit raises the valuation. The largest odd prime of each new index is $q$, its exact $q$-power is $q^e$, and all other odd prime powers divide $t<q$. This proves disjointness, termination and the index bound $q^et\le k(k-1)$. The term at $pk$ uniquely maximizes $m+v_2(m)$, since $2\log_2 k<2k\le pk-k(k-1)$ and $\log_2 k<p$. Its exponent is $pk+v_2(k)$. This is the exact denominator exponent for the constructed group. The optimization over all groups is for the largest original index.

The single group extends to a partition. On each odd row $d>1$ all terms have odd denominator $d$ and unit numerator after dyadic scaling modulo $d$. After any finite deletions, some unit residue occurs infinitely often. Retire the first unused term with at most $d-1$ later copies of that residue and repeat. Every surviving index is eventually covered. Row 1 consists of dyadic terms. This extension supplies no simultaneous cutoff control for other primes.

The distinct-residue estimate uses the fact that each positive lag has at most one colliding pair. With index 1 reserved, the restricted-sum theorem gives the claimed square-root upper bound. For the opposite direction each fixed subset has a positive cleared numerator below $2^kL_k\log 2$. Summing the log sizes over the $2^{k-1}$ subsets containing 1 gives the stated dyadic-interval exception bound. Summing dyadic intervals and selecting a good prime in $((1-\varepsilon)N,N]$ gives the universal lower bound on partition closing indices. The all-prime square-root upper bound and the almost-all logarithmic lower bound have different quantifiers. [Dias da Silva and Hamidoune](https://doi.org/10.1112/blms/26.2.140)

**OP-20.** Let $A_k=\sum_{j\le k}a_j$, $Q_n=\mathrm{den}(2^n A_n)$ and $L_n=\mathrm{lcm}(1,\ldots,n)$. For $p^a\le n<p^{a+1}$ and odd $p$, put $k=\lfloor n/p^a\rfloor$. Modulo $p$, the scaled prefix $p^a2^n A_n$ equals $2^n A_k$. A loss at $p$ requires a zero at $k$ and costs at most $a\log p$. For fixed $k$, the sum of $\log p$ over such $p>k$ is $O(k)$ by the numerator bound.

Two zeros at distance $h$ with both indices at most $K<p$ give a polynomial of degree at most $h-1$ in the starting index. Clearing by $2^h\prod_{i=1}^h(k+i)$ gives value $2^{h-1}(h-1)!$ at $k=-1$, a unit modulo $p$. Hence at most $h-1$ gaps of length $h$ occur. Among consecutive zeros at most $K/H$ gaps exceed $H$ and at most $H(H-1)/2$ are shorter or equal. Taking $H=\lceil K^{1/3}\rceil$ gives $Z_p(K)\ll K^{2/3}$.

Primes up to $X^{3/7}$ cost $O(X^{3/7})$ even pointwise, by $\pi(X^{3/7})\log X$. For larger primes $a\le2$. Exponent two has $k\le X^{1/7}$, with mean cost at most $2\log p/k$ per band. The numerator bound gives $O(X^{1/7})$ in all. For $a=1$ and $p>X^{4/7}$ the same calculation costs $O(X^{3/7})$. In the remaining range each zero accounts for at most $p$ indices. The mean is bounded by a constant times $X^{-1/3}\sum_{p\le X^{4/7}}p^{1/3}\log p\ll X^{-1/3}(X^{4/7})^{4/3}=X^{3/7}$. This proves the stated upper bound on the mean. The explicit $3.5\sqrt X$ bound for $X\ge100$ also holds. For its small-prime step one can use $\sum_{n\le X}\sqrt n\le 2X^{3/2}/3+\sqrt X$.

## Finite certificates and prior art

The author's default script ran through its success message. The $n=3$ exception now passes with $R_3=16/3$ and $L_3/Q_3=2$. The corrected witness description also passes a concrete check. At $q=7$ residue 5 first receives the witness $\{1,2\}$, while $\{3\}$ has the same residue. The cover procedure needs one witness of any size.

* For the million-prime survey I used the common denominator $C=2^{25}L_{25}$ for primes above 25. I formed subset sums of the integer weights $C/(j2^j)$ in two halves, with indices 2–13 and 14–25, reserving index 1. An opposite-residue match with the first possible largest active index gives the exact minimum. Small primes were evaluated in their own fields. This reproduced every bin in §7. The excess extremes are $-9.737165$ at 436967 and $5.188110$ at 920393. The lower witness is $\{1,2,3,5,6,7,8,9\}$. In $(500000,1000000]$ exactly 18 of 36960 primes have $k\le11$.
* For all 2260 constructed groups through 20000, I independently scaled by $2^{pk+v_2(k)}$ and cleared the odd lcm of the indices. In each case the resulting integer divided by that odd lcm was an odd integer. I also checked $m\le k(k-1)$ for every added index. The single-ray and general cutoffs tie at precisely 5, 11, 41 and 61. At 1009 the cutoffs are 1033216 and 8072 respectively. The author script additionally factors the cleared numerators for all subsets through $k=12$ and cross-checks the resulting prime lists against its bitsets.
* A separate modular calculation using successive inverse-base powers and an inverse table reproduced the four block identities through $p<20000$, including each admissible base among 3, 5 and 10. The default script checks all four through 5000. Its logarithm-band checks at 1093 and 3511 produce exponent zero for $a=1$ and exponent one at both reported $a=2$ endpoints for 1093. The latter assertion in code accepts any exponent below two. Tightening it to equality with one would protect the reported number.
* Iterating the rational recurrence $R_n=2R_{n-1}+1/n$ in reduced fractions gave mean losses $2.896504,4.826943,6.690000,7.777954$ through 100, 500, 1000 and 2000. The author's enumeration gives 174 large-prime drop pairs through 300000 and mean contribution 3.7888. Neither finite test determines an asymptotic exponent.

Rogers already studies the very same weighted zero set $J_p(y)$, including $y=1/2$, and discusses the Wieferich connection for $G_{p-1}(2)$. Sanna's Lemma 2.2 proves a short-interval $O(K^{2/3})$ estimate for ordinary harmonic numbers using the same consecutive-gap count and degree bound. His Lemma 3.2 uses the leading base-$p$ digit to detect full denominator survival. The Phase 19 argument adapts the gap polynomial to geometric weights and sums the resulting bounds over varying primes and exponents. Those sources establish clear precedents for the ingredients. Neither cited result states the $3/7$ mean loss or the positive same-cutoff construction. Granville's equation (2) is the order-two congruence conjectured by Skula. The numerical Wieferich search limits should be attributed as dated finite results.
