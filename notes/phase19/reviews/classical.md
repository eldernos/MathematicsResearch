# Independent review of the Phase 19 classical estimates

The finite binary logarithm-row criterion, its two worked spectra, the sharp missing-digit rate and the finite inverse-dilation estimate hold. Two qualifications matter for publication. An earlier Phase 19 review gives a larger lower bound for the sparse factors by counting the same shifts repeatedly. Also, a published preprint already constructs two members of the binary mean-zero subgroup with a normal product. Its proofs give the required digit statistics explicitly.

## The horizontal Taylor rows

Write $Z_d=\sum_{j\ge0}2^{-(d2^j+j)}$ for odd $d\ge1$, and $L_d=Z_d/d$. In a fixed finite family, the normalized leading positions $d/2^{\lfloor\log_2d\rfloor}$ are distinct. Their finite cyclic list has a positive minimum gap, including the jump to twice the first value. Consequently the gaps between consecutive late nonzero insertions are bounded below by a fixed positive multiple of their positions. Early coincidences, for instance between rows 1 and 3, cause no problem. [Logarithm rows](../logarithm-rays.md)

For $r+\sum c_dZ_d$, multiply by a fixed power of two and write the resulting rational coefficients with a common odd denominator $Q$, including the constant. A nonintegral row adds a nonzero residue at each of its late insertions. The adjoining states cannot both vanish, since doubling is invertible modulo $Q$. On the nonzero side, all but a logarithmic number of shifts in a gap of length comparable to its endpoint stay at least $1/(2Q)$ from zero. This supplies infinitely many prefixes with positive mean distance, including for signed coefficients. With integral rows, the sum of the row contributions has mean distance tending to zero, so the rational constant must itself be dyadic. The exceptional shifts number $O_S(\log^2 N)$. In the geometric tail bound the implied constant also depends on the fixed coefficients.

Every empirical limit for a finite combination is supported on the finite grid, and has zero entropy. Rauzy's theorem gives preservation of normality under translation. For transcendence, clear the coefficients to integers. The first omitted nonzero term dominates all later terms in absolute value, even with signs. The reduced dyadic denominator is at most $2^m$ for a truncation at position $m$. The nonzero error is $O(2^{-(1+\eta)m})$ for a fixed $\eta>0$. Rational separation excludes a rational limit. For an algebraic irrational, use Ridout with any exponent strictly between $1$ and $1+\eta$ and denominators supported on $\{2\}$. The conclusions concern fixed finite combinations. Passing to the infinite Taylor sum requires control uniform in the growing collection of rows. [Rauzy, 1975](https://numdam.org/item/AST_1975__24-25__263_0.pdf) · [Bergelson and Downarowicz, §§3–4](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.12929v1) · [Ridout, 1957](https://doi.org/10.1112/S0025579300001182)

The full gap immediately before shell $j$ has asymptotic weight $1/2$, and the $h$th preceding gap has weight $2^{-h}$. The unfinished gap contributes $v=(N-e_j)/N$ to the current cycle. Along each recurring phase the mesh of these $v$ tends to zero and its right endpoint tends to $1/2$. Thus both endpoints, as well as all intermediate mixtures, occur. The cost of bounded cycle remainders and discarded shifts before insertions is $O_d(\log^2(2N)/N)$ in *scalar discrepancy*. Enlarging an arc in each direction proves this claim even when the limiting law has atoms. It does not require total-variation convergence of the actual orbit to a discrete law.

For an independent exact calculation, let $w_i(v)$ be the mass at $i/d$ and put $P_k(v)=\sum_{i<k}(w_i(v)-1/d)$, including $P_0=P_d=0$. The infimum of the lengths of half-open arcs containing a specified forward run of $\ell$ grid points in order is $(\ell-1)/d$, even for $\ell=1$. Taking all cyclic groups gives

$$
D(v)=1/d+\max_k P_k(v)-\min_k P_k(v).
$$

I formed each residue directly as $\sum_{i=0}^j2^{e_j-e_i}\bmod d$, detected its cycle together with $2^j\bmod\operatorname{ord}_d(2)$ for $d>1$, and evaluated this last expression at endpoints and all crossings of the prefix sums. This independently reproduced the supplied calculator for every odd $d\le15$. In increasing order, the pairs of limiting extremes were $(1,1)$, $(2/3,2/3)$, $(1/5,8/15)$, $(16/91,32/63)$, $(2/9,2/9)$, $(1/11,59584/95325)$, $(1/13,2048/4095)$ and $(2/15,2/5)$. For $d=5$ the discrepancy minimum occurs at the uniform grid law, with mass $w_0=1/5$ at the origin. For $d=3$ the nonzero two-cycle gives a limiting worst translation change of $1/3$. Here $d$ indexes the actual summand with its $1/d$ multiplier. [Exact cycle calculator](../../../scripts/phase19_horizontal.py)

## Sharp rates and the scope of the product example

For the missing-digit set $A_S$, fix a backward neighborhood length $L$ and group the indices by their high digits above the smallest power $h^k$ with $rh^k\ge2(h-1)L$. There are $O((N/L)^\sigma)$ relevant clusters of diameter $O(L)$, so the union costs $O(N^\sigma L^{1-\sigma})$ shifts. At least a constant times $(N/L)^\sigma$ noninitial clusters start in $[L,N)$ when $L=o(N)$. The previous cluster ends at least $2L$ earlier. At distances $2$ through $L$ before each such start the next positive digit therefore gives a true circle distance in $[b^{-L},1/3]$. These sets of shifts are disjoint. A short arc hitting a sample near zero misses this entire collection when $L=\lfloor(1-\sigma)\log_b(N)/2\rfloor$. A short arc missing every near-zero sample misses at least half of the mass. The complementary upper bound uses $L=\lceil2\log_bN\rceil$. These estimates give $(\log N/N)^{1-\sigma}$ on all sufficiently large prefixes, with equality of the deficit and worst translation change already at input zero. [Sharp rates](../products-of-remainders.md#sharp-rates-from-missing-digits-in-the-insertion-indices)

There is an exact diagnostic for the conflicting sharpness assertion in the earlier geometry review. For $S=\{0,1\}$ in base four, $r=2$, $N=2\cdot4^t$ and $L=4^k$ with $1\le k<t$, the number of shifts in $[0,N)$ within one through $L$ places before a support point is

$$
2^{t-k}\left(L+\frac{2(4^k-1)}{3}\right).
$$

Within each cluster the largest internal gap is at most $L$, and between clusters the gap exceeds $L$. The cluster at zero loses its initial $L$ sites and the one starting exactly at $N$ restores them. I checked the formula by exact interval unions for all 27 pairs with $3\le t\le8$. The union has order $\sqrt{NL}$. Counting $L$ shifts separately at each of order $\sqrt N$ insertions overstates it. The older $O(\log N/\sqrt N)$ upper bound remains valid, but its claimed matching lower bound in the **Phase 19** review should be superseded. [Earlier geometry review](geometry-periods.md)

Moser's generating series already gives $f(t)f(t^2)=1/(1-t)$ and the unique decomposition $n=a+2a'$. The substitution $t=b^{-r}$ gives the rational product in the note. Its fractional orbit has period exactly $r$ for $r\ge2$ because no earlier positive $b^j-1$ reaches $b^r-1$. Both factors have sparse positive ones, and the preceding rate for each is $\sqrt{\log N/N}$ (for the second factor replace $r$ by $2r$). [Moser, 1962, pp. 37–38](https://oeis.org/A000695/a000695.pdf)

The same gaps also settle the earlier review's question about transcendence. The largest index with at most $k$ base-$h$ digits is $a_k=(\max S)(h^k-1)/(h-1)$. The next index is $a'_k=h^k\min(S\setminus\{0\})$, and $\lim a'_k/a_k>1$ because $\max S\le h-2$. The partial sum has denominator dividing $b^{ra_k}$ and its positive tail is $O_b(b^{-ra'_k})$. Rational separation and Ridout as above imply transcendence of every such $W$. For the two product factors the ratio tends to three, so even the ordinary Roth theorem suffices. [Ridout, 1957](https://doi.org/10.1112/S0025579300001182)

There is a stronger binary precedent for failure of multiplicative closure. In the proof of Theorem 8.11, Bergelson and Downarowicz prove that the frequency of digit switches in $xy$ is zero. In the proof of Proposition 8.13 they prove that the frequency of ones in $1/y$ is zero. For each fixed $L$, all but $o(N)$ shifts of the first number start with $L$ identical digits and those of the second with $L$ zeros. Either property puts the sample within $2^{-L}$ of an integer on a density-one set. Letting $L$ grow proves that both factors belong to the binary mean-zero subgroup $K_2$, and their product $x$ is normal by Corollary 8.14. This inference uses the actual construction, since determinism alone would be insufficient. Grouping runs of ones of $xy$ as differences of dyadic powers even gives a signed expansion with density-zero support. The source does not give two factors with the prescribed positive missing-digit supports of the Phase 19 example. The all-radix rational-product construction and the sharp discrepancy rates have that additional structure. [Bergelson and Downarowicz, §§8.4–8.5](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.12929v1#S8.SS5)

## The inverse bound for integer dilation

Write $\mu$ for the first $N$ samples of $x$ and $\nu$ for their images under $u\mapsto mu$. For a half-open cell $J$ of length at most $1/m$, $\mu(J)\le\nu(mJ)\le m|J|+\beta$ with $\beta=D_N(mx)$. At equality of lengths the image is the entire circle. The grid of size $m$ and all inverse endpoint images up to time $h-1$ produce at most $m+2\sum_{j<h}b^j$ cells, including when endpoints coincide or the test arc wraps. The absolute centered time average is constant on each and at most one. The boundary error of the time average is at most $\sum_{j<h} j/(Nh)=(h-1)/(2N)$.

Under Haar measure, partition the test arc into full cells of size $b^{-j}$ and up to two partial cells. The covariance at lag $j$ is bounded by $2b^{-j}$. Thus the diagonal contributes at most $h/4$ to the variance of the unnormalized sum and the two off-diagonal halves at most $4h/(b-1)$. Cauchy–Schwarz gives precisely the constant in the note. As a separate finite check I evaluated the variance by rational cell integration for all 672 combinations of $b=2,3,4,5$, $h=1,2,3,4$, all seven starting points on the sevenths grid, and all six nontrivial grid lengths. Taking $h=\max(1,\lfloor\frac12\log_b(1/\beta)\rfloor)$ and using $\beta\ge1/N$ proves the claimed rate along any sequence of prefixes with $\beta\to0$, at fixed $b,m$. This upper estimate carries no optimality assertion. The qualitative rational-multiplication theorem applies to normal inputs, while this estimate also compares individual finite prefixes. [Inverse dilation](../geometry.md#an-explicit-inverse-bound-for-dilation) · [Maxfield, 1953](https://msp.org/pjm/1953/3-1/pjm-v3-n1-p13-p.pdf)

## Positive subsets and cancellation at one prime

For an odd prime $p$ write $a_m=1/(m2^m)$. Clearing one factor of $p$ from $a_{jp}$ for $p\nmid j$ gives the residue $u_j=(j2^j)^{-1}\bmod p$ by Fermat. A positive subset containing $a_p$ cancels that simple pole exactly when its other residues sum to $-1/2$. In the range $j<p$ all multiples of $p$ have been accounted for. Terms prime to $p$ may be added without affecting the test. Above that range one must separately control indices divisible by $p^2$. [Phase 18 prime-index obstruction](../../phase18/prime-delay.md)

An independent proof of the square-root upper scale is short. Among $j=2,\ldots,k<p$, two equal residues at a fixed lag $h>0$ require $(2^h-1)j\equiv-h2^h\bmod p$. If the coefficient vanishes this is impossible, otherwise there is at most one pair at that lag. Among $m=k-1$ entries there are therefore at most $m-1$ unordered equal pairs. Cauchy–Schwarz gives at least $m^2/(3m-2)$ distinct residues. For a set of $t$ distinct residues, the sums of $\lfloor t/2\rfloor$ distinct members cover at least $\min(p,1+\lfloor t^2/4\rfloor)$ residues. Taking $k=6\lceil\sqrt p\rceil+1$ thus suffices for all sufficiently large primes. Reserving index 1 before applying this theorem is essential. This proves a local bound using positive terms. [Dias da Silva and Hamidoune, 1994](https://doi.org/10.1112/blms/26.2.140)

For finite verification I split $u_2,\ldots,u_k$ into two halves (skipping $p\mid j$), enumerated each half's subset sums, and tested whether their sumsets hit the reserved target. If $k(p)$ is the first successful $k$, the sums of $k(p)$ over all odd primes through 100, 1,000 and 10,000 were respectively 151, 1,480 and 15,090. The corresponding sample sizes were 24, 167 and 1,228. The maximum through 10,000 was 18, attained only at 8,689. These are finite local statistics.

Three exact witnesses separate the conclusions. At $p=13$ the earliest positive choice is $\{j=1,3\}$. Its sum is $(3\cdot2^{26}+1)/(39\cdot2^{39})$, whose reduced odd denominator is **3**. At $p=7$ the positive minimum is 6, but allowing signs already cancels the pole at 3 with $u_1+u_2-u_3=0$. Finally $1/3!-1/4!=1/8$ illustrates why the earlier factorial impossibility for $e$ explicitly assumes positive terms. A signed relation needs a separate identity or partition before it describes the original positive series. A global finite dyadic group has both an original completion index and a reduced dyadic exponent, which can differ across rays.

### Lifting local cancellation at the same original cutoff

For a **single prescribed prime** $p\ge5$, the least possible last index of a finite positive dyadic Taylor group containing $a_p$ is exactly $p\,k(p)$. This sharpens the distinction illustrated by $p=13$. The local subset can retain other poles, but additional terms from the original Taylor series can always remove them before that cutoff.

First $k(p)<p$. The ratio $u_2/u_3=3$ is distinct from both $1$ and $-1$ modulo $p$, so the first two available indices, 2 and 3, have four distinct subset sums. Each remaining nonzero $u_j$, $4\le j<p$, increases the attained cardinality by at least one until it reaches $p$. Indeed translation by a nonzero residue cannot stabilize a proper nonempty subset of $\mathbb F_p$. Thus $-u_1$ is reached. Before cutoff $p\,k(p)$ all indices divisible by $p$ have the form $jp$ with $j<k(p)<p$ and provide no such subset. This proves necessity even if arbitrary positive off-axis terms are admitted.

For sufficiency the following argument works for any positive witness $S$ with $k=\max S<p$. Keep every $a_{jp}$, $j\in S$. The odd denominator of their sum divides $L_k=\operatorname{lcm}(1,\ldots,k)$ after the local cancellation. Process odd primes $q\le k$ in decreasing order. At prime $q$ process $e=\lfloor\log_q k\rfloor,\ldots,1$. If the current sum is $T$ with $q^eT$ integral at $q$, the candidate original terms are

$$
a_{q^e t},\qquad 1\le t<q.
$$

Multiplication by $q^e$ makes all $q-1$ residues nonzero modulo $q$. Starting with $\{0\}$, the same translation argument shows that their subset sums fill $\mathbb F_q$. Choose a subset summing to $-q^eT\bmod q$ after scaling. The new sum has valuation at least $1-e$. Its new terms have largest odd prime $q$, exact $q$-valuation $-e$, and every other odd prime power in their denominators divides $t<q$. Thus no step reintroduces a cleared larger prime, each remaining odd prime power is at most $k$, and the candidate pools are pairwise disjoint and avoid the original multiples of $p$. At the end the sum is dyadic. Every added index is at most $k(k-1)<pk$.

For this construction the unique greatest dyadic exponent among its terms is $pk+v_2(k)$. Earlier original multiples of $p$ lie at least $p$ places below $pk$ and have two-adic valuation at most $\log_2 k$. For the added terms, $m+v_2(m)\le k(k-1)+2\log_2 k<pk$. The last numerator is odd at the greatest exponent, so the resulting group retires at exactly $pk+v_2(k)$. Minimizing retirement over arbitrary groups and achieving individual minima simultaneously in a single partition require further arguments. A single specified finite group does extend to a partition: on each affected odd row reserve an unused constant-residue tail progression, use disjoint finite parts of it to absorb each of the finitely many surviving initial terms, and partition each remaining progression into full zero-sum blocks as in Phase 18.

I checked the construction with exact rational sums for all 93 primes $5\le p\le499$. For $p=13$, the indices $\{3,6,13,39\}$ give $24343310231/2^{39}$. For $p=7$, $\{3,6,7,15,21,35,42\}$ gives $399245531709/2^{43}$. The exceptional prime $p=3$ has least last index 12: before then a term at 9 would be the unique double pole, and the indices 3 and 6 have equal nonzero residues at the simple pole. The pair $\{3,12\}$ sums to $683/2^{14}$.

## A refinement of the average denominator-loss estimate

Put $A_k=\sum_{j=1}^k1/(j2^j)$, $R_n=2^nA_n$, and let $Q_n$ be its reduced denominator. The mean of $\log(L_n/Q_n)$ for $1\le n\le X$ is $O(X^{3/7})$. This bound compares reduced rational denominators on average. Its proof uses neither a probability model for numerators nor an assumption about Wieferich primes.

Fix an odd prime $p$ and let $Z_p(K)$ count the zeros of $A_k\bmod p$ for $1\le k\le K<p$. Zeros at both $k$ and $k+h$ imply

$$
\sum_{i=1}^h \frac{2^{-i}}{k+i}=0\bmod p.
$$

Clearing the linear denominators gives a polynomial in $k$ of degree at most $h-1$. Its value at $-1$ is $2^{-1}(h-1)!\ne0\bmod p$ since $h<p$. Thus at most $h-1$ pairs of zeros have lag $h$. Among consecutive zeros, the number of gaps longer than $H$ is at most $K/H$, and the number of shorter gaps is at most $\sum_{h\le H}(h-1)$. Taking $H=\lceil K^{1/3}\rceil$ gives $Z_p(K)\ll K^{2/3}$ uniformly. Related work on unweighted modular harmonic sums provides context. [Garaev, Luca and Shparlinski, 2006](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00209-006-0939-5)

If $p^a\le n<p^{a+1}$, put $k=\lfloor n/p^a\rfloor$. Multiplying $R_n$ by $p^a$ and reducing modulo $p$ gives $2^n A_k$ by Fermat. Consequently loss at $p$ requires a zero at $k$, and costs at most $a\log p$. For any fixed $k$, the sum of $\log p$ over such primes $p>k$ is $O(k)$, since the positive reduced numerator of $A_k$ is at most $2^kL_k$ and $\log L_k=O(k)$. A band with given $a,k,p$ contains at most $p^a$ values of $n$. Four bounds now suffice:

1. Primes $p\le X^{3/7}$, including 2, together cost at most $\pi(X^{3/7})\log X=O(X^{3/7})$ pointwise.
2. For larger primes, $a\le2$. If $a=2$ then $k\le X^{1/7}$. The mean contribution of a band is at most $2p^2\log p/X\le2\log p/k$. Summing the numerator bound over $k$ costs $O(X^{1/7})$.
3. If $a=1$ and $p>X^{4/7}$ then $k\le X^{3/7}$. The same calculation costs $O(X^{3/7})$.
4. For all remaining primes with $a=1$, each $p$ contributes at most $(p\log p/X)Z_p(\min(p-1,\lfloor X/p\rfloor))$ to the mean. The sum is at most a constant times $X^{-1/3}\sum_{p\le X^{4/7}}p^{1/3}\log p=O(X^{3/7})$ by the Chebyshev bound.

This proves the stated exponent as an upper bound. It gives no asymptotic equivalent for the loss, no sharpness result and no bound on the discrepancy of $\log 2$.
