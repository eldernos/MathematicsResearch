# Independent review of the Stoneham discrepancy rates

For a degree-$d$ polynomial on an odd-prime-power axis, the deduction at `648fb5a` gives $D_N\le C N^{-1/(2(2^{d+1}-2))}$ for every $N\ge2$, with computable $C$. The proof also covers the stated combinations with one leading monomial. The square and cube exponents, including their logarithmic factors, check out. Vandehey’s auxiliary divisor must use the primes actually dividing the reduced modulus. Doubling the Erdős–Turán constant covers the cutoff convention in Lean. ## Which exponential sums are covered

Vandehey defines the allowed moduli to include 1. Theorem 5.1 bounds the **absolute sum** from 1 through any positive integer length, for every reduced numerator. The fixed base is coprime to the support. Lemma 2.2 by itself has a multiplicative-order length restriction. Lemma 2.3 removes it by counting full periods and a remainder, and Proposition 4.1 preserves the all-length quantifier at each iteration. For a reduced starting value $a/q$ with $q>1$, choose $a' b\equiv a\pmod q$. The terms with indices $1,\ldots,L$ and numerator $a'$ are exactly the desired terms with indices $0,\ldots,L-1$. For $q=1$ the sum equals $L$, covered by $B_k\ge1$ and $\nu_k\ge1$. This includes an integer starting value and a frequency divisible by the whole denominator. [Vandehey, §§1–2, Proposition 4.1 and Theorem 5.1](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1606.07911)

There is a literal issue in the preprint’s proof at (12). For example, if the allowed primes are 3 and 5 and the modulus is a power of 3, using their product 15 in (12) puts a factor 5 in the claimed divisor $m'\mid m$. At that step use $\prod_{p\mid m}p$ and apply the special factor for 4 precisely when $4\mid m$. This gives (13) with the full support product as an upper bound. To carry the induction through smaller supports, take common upper bounds at the previous depth and maximize the new constants over the finitely many actual supports. Formula (3) uses a finite multiplicative order and integer valuations, and Lemma 4.2 uses a finite product. Hence the constants depend effectively on $b,S,k$, uniformly even when multiplying by a frequency removes a prime altogether. [Vandehey, pp. 4, 9–10 and 13–14](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1606.07911#page=10)

## From characters to intervals and words

At the start of a piece let $Q$ be the reduced prefix denominator. For a positive frequency $h$, set $g=\gcd(h,Q)$. The two denominator factors after reduction are at most $Q^\alpha$ and exactly $Q^{-\alpha}g^\alpha$, respectively. Grouping by $g$ costs at most $(1+\log H)\sum_{g\mid Q}g^{\alpha-1}$. Each local geometric series is bounded by $(1-p^{\alpha-1})^{-1}$, uniformly in its length, because $\alpha\le1/2$. This permits $H$ to grow. Bailey–Crandall’s Theorem 2.2(9) uses positive frequencies, normalized sums and $1/H$. Multiplying by the sample size $T$ and using $1/H\le2/(H+1)$ gives Lean’s `ErdosTuran` with twice the published absolute constant. One can also bound the absolute constant directly. The degree-$H$ Selberg interval majorant and minorant have mean error $1/(H+1)$ and Fourier coefficients at $1\le h\le H$ of modulus at most $1/(\pi h)+1/(H+1)$. Pairing conjugate frequencies gives, for example, $C=4$ in this form of the inequality. Lean’s `disc` uses the fractional parts and half-open intervals, and `phase` is $e^{2\pi i x}$. With $s=\prod_{p\in S}p$, the condition $q\mid s^j$ for some $j$ in `FixedSupportBound` is exactly the required support condition. [Bailey–Crandall, Definition 2.1 and Theorem 2.2(9)](https://www.davidhbailey.com/dhbpapers/bcnormal-em.pdf#page=4) · [Finite transfer](../../../NormalityChecker/QuantitativeStonehamDiscrepancy.lean) · [Interval convention](../../../NormalityChecker/CircleDiscrepancy.lean)

For $1\le T\le X$, split $[X,X+T)$ at interior insertion positions. A piece starts **after incorporating** every tuple at its left endpoint. Its last index precedes the next insertion. For an insertion of degree $a$, one coordinate contributes between $X/a$ and $2X$, leaving boundedly many exponents in that coordinate and $O((1+\log X)^{a-1})$ tuples in total. An omitted tuple has some $q_i^{j_i}\ge(n+1)/\sum_i m_i$, so the geometric tail in that coordinate is $O(1/(n+1))$ even immediately before an insertion. Summing the phase error with weight $1/h$ costs at most $2\pi KHT/X$. These arguments include a singleton final piece. With $J$ pieces of lengths $L_j$, the two length sums are bounded by $J^{1-\gamma}T^\gamma$ and $T^\nu$. For each test interval one can therefore sum **count errors** over completed shells and the last partial shell, including $n=0$ separately. For a shell estimate $O(X^{1-\eta}(1+\log X)^\ell)$ the geometric ratio is $2^{1-\eta}\ge\sqrt2$. This bounds the sum by a fixed multiple of the last scale, uniformly in the interval, before division by $N$. A word of length $w$ and integer value $m$ selects $[m/b^w,(m+1)/b^w)$, including leading zeroes. Counting only starts wholly inside a prefix of $N$ digits drops at most $w-1$ starts. I substituted $k=d-1$ in Theorem 5.1. The columns $u=1-\gamma-d\alpha$ and $v=1-\nu+d\alpha$ are the two savings before taking the Fourier cutoff. The logarithmic exponent is $1+2^{-k}+(d-1)(1-\gamma)$.

For general $k$, put $s_k=(1-\gamma_k)/\alpha_k$ and $t_k=(\nu_k-1)/\alpha_k$. Equation (15) gives $s_k-t_k=4-2^{1-k}$. Substitution in the recurrence gives $s_{k+1}=s_k+1-\alpha_k t_k$. Since $\nu_k\ge1$, $s_k\le k+2$. Thus $t_0=t_1=0$ and $0\le t_j\le j-1$ for $j\ge2$. The accumulated loss before depth $k$ is strictly less than $\sum_{j\ge2}(j-1)2^{-j-1}=1/2$. It follows that $u=\alpha_k(s_k-d)>\alpha_k/2$ and $v=\alpha_k(d-t_k)\ge\alpha_k$. With $\eta=\min(1/2,u,v)$, $H=\lfloor X^\eta\rfloor$ meets both the truncation and the real-tail costs. The strict margin over $\delta_d=\alpha_k/2$ absorbs the logarithm. For $z=\log N$, the maximum of $(1+z)^\ell e^{-(\eta-\delta_d)z}$ on $z\ge0$ occurs at $\max(0,\ell/(\eta-\delta_d)-1)$. ## Effective denominator and shell cutoffs

The zero-index product with dilations $m_i q_i$ equals the positive-index product times $\prod_i q_i$, including at the prefix level by reindexing the same finite tuples. A common power of $p$ clears these indexing factors across all monomials. In the resulting integral zero-index model let $c_*$ be its nonzero leading coefficient, $J_0$ the sum of its leading monomial’s dilations and $W=\sum_i e_i$ for that monomial. Search $L>J_0$ until every upper subset of the finite box containing the constant anchor $J_0$ has nonzero mass modulo $p^L$, using the weights $p^{\sum_i e_i v_i}$. Here the box is $\{0,\ldots,L-1\}^d$. Each such subset extends by upward closure with exactly the original trace. Every point outside the box contributes zero modulo $p^L$. Uniform precision supplies a passing value, and restriction to its box shows that every larger precision passes. The search therefore terminates. Reading the proof of `retained_denominator` at the reviewed commit gives the sufficient cutoff

```math
\lfloor\log_p n\rfloor \ge \max\{\max(2L,J_0)(W+1),\ L+W+v_p(|c_*|)+1\}.
```

The second term makes the surviving top pole strictly larger than every lower-degree pole. Dividing out the common indexing factor preserves the reduced denominator as a divisor. For rational polynomial coefficients first clear the part of their common denominator supported on the radix primes by shifting $t$ places. The two length-$N$ windows differ by at most $t$ in any interval count. Division by the remaining coprime part enlarges the denominator by at most that fixed factor. Thus every late start has $Q\asymp X^d$ and fixed support, and the initial positions have a computable trivial count bound. For a single generator with arbitrary coprime $c$, the last insertion gives a unit modulo $c$ after clearing $c^{\lfloor\log_c n\rfloor}$. This proves the degree-one affine extension for nonzero slope. The published exponential-sum constants, the effective absolute Erdős–Turán constant from its trigonometric majorants, and the finite operations above suffice to compute a valid $C$. For the unequal-slope variant write $c_- X^\lambda\le Q\le c_+ X^D$. At the proposed cutoff $h\le X^\beta$ one has $q\ge c_-X^{\lambda-\beta}$. For $X\ge\max(1,c_+^{2/D})$, every piece of length at least $X^{1/2}$ satisfies $L\ge q^{1/(3D)}$, exactly the length condition of Corollary 1.2. The shorter pieces together cost at most $JX^{1/2}$ counts. The rational exponent intervals of §6.1 give an effective saving. Cover the range from a positive rational $\varepsilon<\min(1,1/(3D))$ up to 1 by finitely many closed intervals inside their interiors, and take the smallest endpoint margin from Lemma 6.2. Lengths $L\ge q$ use Lemma 2.3 directly. With $\delta$ the resulting modulus exponent, the four normalized errors have power savings $\beta$, $1/2$, $\theta-\beta$ and $\delta(\lambda-\beta)$ before logarithms. Each is positive for the note’s $\beta=\min(1,\lambda,\theta)/2$. Half their minimum absorbs the logarithms effectively. In the stated composite application $v_p(c)>0$ supplies $\lambda=d\log_c(p^{v_p(c)})$, while $c^{d\lfloor\log_c n\rfloor}$ supplies the upper bound. Under the stipulated local convergence, a stabilization cutoff at any required precision can be found by iterating the power map modulo $p^L$ until two successive residues agree. ## Scope of the evidence

I checked the Lean source statements at `648fb5a`. The finite transfer takes the exponential-sum and Erdős–Turán bounds as explicit arguments. The dyadic lemma takes an estimate for **each** partial shell. The exponent lemma takes a rational recurrence, and the real-tail theorem concerns the zero-index product. The application to Stoneham values, prime-support product bound, identification of the external exponents, and algorithm for a uniform constant are paper deductions. This was a source audit without a fresh Lean build. The historical comparisons have compatible scopes. For $r>1$, Stoneham’s Theorem 3 gives $2/\varphi(p^r)$ for a full rational period under its primitive-root, radix and block-length conditions. In Kano (13), setting $a=c$, $\lambda_n=n$ and $\mu_n=c^n$ gives a normalized bound $O(N^{-\log_c(2)/2}+N^{-1/2}(1+\log N)^2)$ for the single generator. Discarding the first $\lceil\sqrt N\rceil$ starts and moving the other interval endpoints by $O(N^{-1/2})$ gives that rate for the real orbit as well.
